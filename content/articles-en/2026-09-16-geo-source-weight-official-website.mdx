## The short answer

“Is GEO just getting company information into enough news stories, newsletters, and social posts for AI to summarize?” Distribution is part of the picture. The more useful question is what the buyer asked and which accessible source can support the answer.

I looked at the public guidance from Google, Bing, and OpenAI. None publishes a general split such as “company website 50%, news 30%.” Nor is there one shared GEO score across AI products. A count of visible citations does not reveal that split. Bing explicitly says its AI Performance citation count is a measure of frequency, not ranking, authority, prominence, or a page's role in an answer. [Bing's explanation of the metric](https://blogs.bing.com/webmaster/February-2026/Introducing-AI-Performance-in-Bing-Webmaster-Tools-Public-Preview)

## Different questions need different sources

Suppose a buyer asks, “What does this company offer now, and where does a person still approve the work?” The current [services page](/en/services) should state the company's position. A two-year-old announcement can establish what was announced then; it may no longer describe the service boundary today. If the buyer asks whether the company has independent press coverage, its own press release cannot establish that. The original article and its publication date matter.

A public account post or independent creator's article might add first-hand experience or industry context. Before treating it as evidence, check who wrote it, when it appeared, and whether it points to an original record. Ten accounts reposting one announcement do not create ten independent observations. Access also varies by product and page. We cannot assume every AI service can retrieve a particular social or messaging-platform post.

This is an editorial way to inspect evidence, not a disclosed ranking formula. The eventual answer also depends on whether a page is retrievable, how well it matches the question, how current it is, and the product's own source selection. Google says its AI Search features may run several related searches around a question. A supporting link must be indexed and eligible for a normal Search snippet, yet eligibility does not guarantee that it will appear. [Google's guidance for AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features) OpenAI says publicly available websites can appear in ChatGPT Search in its [publisher FAQ](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/12627856); its [search guide](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/9237897-chatgpt-search) says ranking involves multiple factors and placement is not guaranteed.

## The company's site is a reference point

A company can keep its own site current: name, service scope, conditions, contact details, and links to supporting evidence. It cannot edit every outside article. Reports age, and summaries can drop qualifications. The site gives buyers and outside writers a place to check the company's present account.

Ownership alone does not earn a citation. A page that says only “end-to-end intelligent solutions” still fails a buyer who asks what will be delivered and which decisions remain with people. An industry article that explains the work clearly may be more useful for that particular question. Google's content guidance favors original information, clear sourcing, and material that helps readers. It does not give a company domain an automatic pass. [Google's helpful-content guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content)

Consider a conflict: a current service page says a human approves important external sends, while an older article calls the process “fully automated.” For today's service terms, check the dated site statement and the actual terms. For the history of the announcement, keep the article. The disagreement deserves investigation; counting how many pages repeat each version will not settle it.

## Where this reasoning stops

The source roles above are a way to check claims. Google's, Bing's, and OpenAI's guidance applies to their respective products. It cannot be combined into a universal weighting formula. A well-maintained site is not promised indexing, citations, or recommendations. Whether a post behind a login or on a particular platform is accessible needs to be tested with the actual product and URL.

## A practical check

Choose one real buyer question, such as “Which steps can the provider handle, and which decisions stay with a person?” List the site pages, original news pieces, and social posts that appear to answer it. Record the author, date, and original URL. Read what each source can actually establish, marking reposts and conflicting claims.

Then return to the site. Can a buyer find the current capability, its conditions, and a path to evidence on the relevant page? Our [project pages](/en/projects) and services page have different jobs: the former describe published project facts; the latter explains how the service works. If a claim requires independent endorsement, link to the outside source rather than recasting the company's own description as third-party validation.

## What to record

Ask the same question in the AI product you care about. Save the question, time, answer, and cited URLs. Record “the page loads,” “the page is cited,” and “the answer is accurate” as separate observations. Bing exposes cited pages and related queries in AI Performance, which can help track changes. One answer, or one product's citation count, cannot disclose its hidden allocation across channels. [Bing AI Performance](https://www.bing.com/webmasters/help/ai-performance-9f8e7d6c)
